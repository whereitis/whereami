whereami

Where does the S&P 500 stand in its own history? Each reading uses only the data a person had on that day. No hindsight refits.

price only total return (dividends reinvested) dashed lines: plus and minus 1 and 2 standard deviations

This is a gauge, not a signal. Tested as seen on the day, this reading has shown no useful link to what the market did over the next 1, 3, 5 or 10 years. High readings have stayed high for years.

"Standard deviations" here is a ruler for the typical size of the gap, not a probability. The gap is a slow cycle, and half of any gap has taken about seven years to close on average. The percentile makes no bell-curve promise: it just counts past months with a smaller gap.

How the reading is made

  • Take the S&P 500 level, adjusted for inflation with the CPI (all urban consumers, not seasonally adjusted).
  • Fit a straight line through the logarithm of that level against time. A straight line in log space is a constant yearly growth rate.
  • For each date, fit the line and its typical gap (sigma) using only data up to that date. The reading is that date's gap divided by that sigma. The percentile is the share of earlier months in the same fit with a smaller gap.
  • Once a reading is published it is never recomputed. Each day's run only adds new dates, and it refuses to run at all if a recomputed old reading would drift.
  • Monthly points from 1950 to about ten years ago, daily points since. Readings begin in 1970 so every fit has at least twenty years behind it.
  • Price only uses the index level. Total return adds dividends reinvested, from Shiller's monthly data until 1987 and the official total-return index from 1988.
